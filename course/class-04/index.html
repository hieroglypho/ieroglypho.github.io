Two- and Three-Consonant Signs

Most Egyptian words aren't spelled letter by letter. Scribes reached for bigger signs that stand for two or three consonants at once, then added an echo or two to make the reading certain. This week you learn the signs you'll meet most often, and the two words behind “Pharaoh” and “Memphis”.

2One sign, two sounds

Twelve signs worth learning first.

An alphabet of 25 letters would have been enough to write every Egyptian word. Scribes never limited themselves to it. Alongside the one-consonant signs they used about a hundred signs that each stand for a pair of consonants. Egyptologists call them two-consonant signs, or biliterals.

You've met a few already: the game board mn in Amun's name, the house pr and the duck zꜣ in Class 1. Here are twelve of the most common, each with a word it often writes.

The table lists twelve two-consonant signs. The house is per. The game board is men. The basket without a handle is neb. The face is her. The head in profile is tep. The eye is ir. The swallow is wer. The pintail duck is sa. The three fox skins are mes. The djed pillar is djed. The wooden column is aa. The strip of land is ta.

Two look-alike pairs. The basket without a handle is nb; the basket with a handle is the letter k. And the duck zꜣ is not the vulture ꜣ, even though its second sound is the vulture's. Look at the neck and the tail: the duck's tail ends in a long point.

One more thing about the duck. In Class 1 we followed Horapollo and called it a goose. Egyptologists identify it as a pintail duck, and that's what this course calls it from now on.

3One sign, three sounds

Six signs that each carry a whole word.

A smaller group of signs stands for three consonants. These three-consonant signs, or triliterals, are usually tied to one particular word and its relatives, so learning the sign means learning the word. You've already seen four of them.

The table lists six three-consonant signs. The sandal strap, the ankh, is ankh, life. The heart and windpipe is nefer, good. The loaf on a reed mat is hetep, offering or be content. The scarab beetle is kheper, come into being. The cow's ear is sedjem, hear. The cloth wound on a pole is netjer, god.

The flag-like sign is new. It probably shows a pole wrapped in cloth, like the flagpoles that stood at temple gateways, and it means “god”. It's often followed by the seated god you know from Class 1, which tags divine words.

From now on, when you meet a sign, ask yourself first how many sounds it stands for: one, two or three. The next game trains exactly that.

4Picture or sound?

The same sign can show a thing or just lend its sounds.

In Class 1 you sorted signs into ideograms, which mean what they show, and phonograms, which stand for sounds. The big signs work both ways. With a stroke under it, the house means “house”. In , “go out”, it only lends its sounds p + r.

Most of the time a big sign is used for its sound. A swallow has nothing to do with greatness, but its name sounded like wr, so means “great”. A duck has nothing to do with sons, but is zꜣ “son”. The fox skins spell ms “child” for the same reason. Egyptians loved this kind of sound-play, and it's the engine of the whole script.

ḏd “stable, enduring”
ḏd “say”

It also works the other way round: the same sounds can be written with different signs, and the choice tells you which word is meant. Both words above read ḏd. The pillar spells “stable”; the cobra and hand from Class 3 spell “say”. You never have to guess: the spelling and the determinative show which one you're reading.

5How a spelling is built

Big sign, echo, tag: the pattern behind most words.

Last week you learned to spot phonetic complements, the one-consonant signs that echo a big sign. Now that you know the big signs, you can see the whole pattern. A typical Egyptian word is built like this:

  1. Sound-signs, often a two- or three-consonant sign with one or two complements after it;
  2. a determinative at the end, which is not read.

The complements usually repeat the last sound of the big sign, or the last two: wr, nfr. A few words repeat every sound. In ꜥꜣ “great”, the column is normally followed by both the forearm ꜥ and the vulture ꜣ. However many echoes there are, you read the sounds once.

This gives you a reading method that works even on signs you haven't learned:

  1. Set aside the determinative at the end.
  2. Find the big sign.
  3. Read the echo after it. It tells you how the big sign ends, even if you've never seen it before.
  4. Read any other sound-signs as letters.

7Case file: the Great House

An architect, a sage, a capital city and a royal title.

Last week you read the name of Ptah, the god of Memphis, the capital of the pyramid age. This week's case file stays in that city. Its people gave their children names that were short sentences, and many of those sentences used the big signs you've just learned.

The file ends with a word you use already. The ancient Egyptians had a way of talking about their king without naming him, and it reached English through the Bible.

Tip: the tiles now include whole values like ḥtp as well as single letters. Give each big sign its full value, and each echo its single letter.
