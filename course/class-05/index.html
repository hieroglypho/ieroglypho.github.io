2The silent signs

Twelve determinatives that do most of the work.

You've been setting determinatives aside since Class 1: the walking legs, the seated man, the sky. This week you stop skipping them and start using them. A determinative is never pronounced and never transliterated, but it's the most helpful sign in the word. It tells you what kind of thing the word is about. And because it stands at the end, it shows you where one word stops and the next begins.

Egyptologists count well over a hundred determinatives. You don't need them all. The twelve below turn up constantly, and you've met more than half of them already.

The table lists twelve determinatives. The seated man tags men, their names and their jobs. The seated woman tags women. The seated god tags gods, and sometimes the king. The man with his hand to his mouth tags eating, drinking, speaking, thinking and feeling. The walking legs tag movement. The man striking with a stick tags force and effort. The house tags buildings and rooms. The town sign, a circle with crossing streets, tags towns and cities. The three hills tag deserts and foreign lands. The sun tags light, days and seasons. The papyrus roll tags writing, and ideas you can't draw. The sparrow tags small, weak or bad things.

Three things to notice. The house and the sun are old friends doing a second job: as sound-signs they are pr and rꜥ, as determinatives they are silent. The papyrus roll is the scribes' answer to a real problem. You can draw a boat or a fish, but how do you draw “know” or “health”? You draw a book. And the sparrow is the one bird in the script with a bad reputation.

Many other determinatives explain themselves, because they are simply pictures: a boat after “boat”, a fish after “fish”, a tree after the name of a tree. The Egyptian word for “cat” is mjw, and it ends, of course, in a cat: .

3Let the tag tell you

Guess the kind of word before you know the word.

Here is a word you haven't met in this course: . You may not know what it means, but you know more than you think. The pool is š and the owl is m. The walking legs tell you it has to do with movement. It is šm, “go”. Reading a real text often works like this: the sound-signs give you the consonants, the determinative narrows down the meaning, and the dictionary does the rest.

Determinatives matter most when two words sound the same. Because vowels weren't written, many different words share the same consonants. The tag at the end tells them apart.

mn “remain, endure”
mn “be ill, suffer”

The game board and its echo spell the same two consonants both times. With the papyrus roll, the word is about the idea of lasting. With the sparrow, something has gone wrong: the word means “be ill”.

ms “child”
msj “give birth”

The same goes for the fox skins. With a child after them they spell “child”. With a woman giving birth they spell the verb “to bear”.

The house sign shows the whole range in one family of words.

WrittenReadsMeansThe last sign says
prhousea stroke: the house means “house”
prjgo outwalking legs: movement
prtPeret, the season of growingthe sun: a word about time

The table shows three words built on the house sign. With a stroke under it, it is the word house. With the walking legs after it, it means go out. With the sun after it, it is the name of a season, the season of growing, because the sun tags words about time.

A few more facts complete the picture. Some words carry two determinatives: Memphis has a pyramid and a town. Some carry none. The short grammar words, like m “in” and n “for”, never have one. And the same word can be tagged a little differently from one scribe to the next, because a determinative was a reading aid, not a fixed part of the spelling.

4Scribes' habits

More than one, and gods before everyone.

Two habits of Egyptian scribes matter in every royal inscription you will ever read. Neither changes how a word sounds. Both change how it looks on the wall.

More than one

To show that a word is plural, scribes added three strokes after it, either or . The older and grander way was to write the sign itself three times. Both of these mean “gods”:

nṯrw “gods”, the sign written three times
nṯrw “gods”, with plural strokes

The plural of most masculine words ends in w, and the strokes stand for that ending: nṯr “god”, nṯrw “gods”. You'll study plurals properly in Class 6. For now, read three strokes as “more than one” and add a w.

Two of something had its own form, the dual, written by doubling the sign. One strip of land is tꜣ, “land”. Two strips are tꜣwj, “the Two Lands”: . This was the Egyptians' name for their own country, the Nile valley in the south and the Delta in the north, joined under one king.

Gods and kings come first

In Class 3 you saw Amun's name written at the front of Tutankhamun's cartouche, although it is read last. That wasn't a one-off. Whenever a phrase contains the word “god”, the word “king” or the name of a god, scribes liked to write that word first, out of respect. You then read the phrase in its real order. Egyptologists call this honorific transposition.

You've known an example since Class 1. The Egyptian name for hieroglyphs is written : the flag that means “god”, then a walking stick, which reads mdw, “words”. On the wall the god stands first. In speech he comes second: mdw-nṯr, “the god's words”.

So when a phrase starts with a god or a king and makes no sense in that order, try reading that word last. But don't swap blindly. In the name Amenhotep, “Amun is content”, the god really does come first, because the sentence starts with him.

5The gods by name

Twelve names, most of them spelled with signs you know.

A god's name is written like any other word: sound-signs first, then a determinative, usually the seated god. You've read Amun, Ptah, Anubis and Sobek this way already. A few of the greatest gods were so familiar that one sign was enough: a falcon for Horus, an ibis for Thoth, the sun itself for Ra.

The table lists twelve gods and how their names are written. Amun, the Hidden One, god of Thebes. Ra, the sun. Amun-Ra, the two joined as king of the gods. Ptah, the craftsman god of Memphis. Horus, the falcon, god of kingship. Osiris, king of the dead, whose holy city was Abydos. Isis, his wife, the mother of Horus. Thoth, the ibis, god of writing. Montu, the war god of Thebes. Khonsu, the moon god, son of Amun. Geb, the earth. And Maat, the goddess of order, truth and justice, who wears a feather on her head.

Two of these spellings are old and fixed, and worth learning whole. Osiris is a throne over an eye. Isis is the same throne with a loaf and an egg. One new sound-sign appears in Khonsu's name: the sedge plant , which reads sw. You'll need it again very soon, in the word for “king”.

Gods matter for reading kings, because most kings were named after one. Amenhotep is “Amun is content”. Mentuhotep says the same of Montu. Thutmose is “Thoth is born”, and Ramesses is “Ra is the one who bore him”. Once you can read the gods, you can read the people named after them.

6The five names of a king

One king, five names, two cartouches.

From the Middle Kingdom on, a king of Egypt had five names. Four he took on the day he came to the throne. One he had carried since birth. Each name follows its own title, and once you know the five titles you can find your way around any royal inscription.

TitleReadsThe name that follows
ḤrwHorus name. The oldest of the five: the king is the god Horus on earth. It is usually written in a frame that shows a palace wall, with the falcon perched on top.
nbtjTwo Ladies name. The vulture goddess of the south and the cobra goddess of the north protect the king.
bjk nbwGold Falcon name. A falcon standing on the sign for gold.
nswt-bjtThrone name, in a cartouche. “He of the sedge and the bee”: king of Upper and Lower Egypt.
zꜣ RꜥBirth name, in a cartouche. “Son of Ra”.

The table lists the five names and the title that comes before each. First, the Horus name, the oldest of the five: the king is the god Horus on earth. Second, the Two Ladies name: the vulture goddess of the south and the cobra goddess of the north protect the king. Third, the Gold Falcon name, written with a falcon standing on the sign for gold. Fourth, the throne name, in a cartouche. Its title means he of the sedge and the bee, the king of Upper and Lower Egypt. Fifth, the birth name, also in a cartouche. Its title means son of Ra.

The last two are the ones that count. Only they are written inside a cartouche, the loop of rope, knotted at one end, that protects the name inside it. The throne name is the one Egyptians themselves used for their king, and it almost always says something about Ra. The birth name is the one we use today: Thutmose, Amenhotep, Ramesses. The numbers are ours. No Egyptian ever said “Thutmose the Third”. To them he was Menkheperre.

The title of the throne name looks odd until you remember section 4. The sedge reads sw. Written in full, the word for “king” is : a sedge, a loaf and a ripple of water, which spell s-w-t-n. But the word is read nswt. It means “he of the sedge”, and the sedge, the emblem of the south, is written first out of respect, like the name of a god. The bee, bjt, stands for the north. Sedge and bee together make the king of the whole country.

Here are all five names of one king, Senwosret I, who ruled around 1950 BC, when Middle Egyptian was the language people spoke. He kept things simple: his first three names are the same.

TitleNameReadsMeans
ꜥnḫ-mswt“Living of births”, three times over
Ḫpr-kꜣ-RꜥKheperkare: “The ka of Ra comes into being”
Z-n-WsrtSenwosret: “Man of the goddess Wosret”

The table shows the five names of Senwosret the First. His Horus name, his Two Ladies name and his Gold Falcon name are all the same, and mean living of births. His throne name is Kheperkare, the ka of Ra comes into being. His birth name is Senwosret, man of the goddess Wosret.

Two new signs appear in his cartouches. The pair of raised arms is kꜣ, the life force that every person and every god was thought to have. The animal's head and neck is wsr, “powerful”. Both names also follow the rule of gods first. Ra is written at the front of the throne name and read at the end. In the birth name the goddess Wosret is written first, and the two small signs for “man of” come after her.

His names are carved on his obelisk at Heliopolis, the oldest obelisk still standing in Egypt, in what is now a suburb of Cairo.

7Inside and around the cartouche

A small stock of words builds most royal names.

Throne names look forbidding, but they are short sentences about Ra, built from a surprisingly small stock of words. You learned most of them last week as big signs.

The table lists the words that build most throne names. Ra, written first and read last. Enduring. Form, or coming into being. Perfect. Great. Lord. Stable. Appear in glory, like the sun rising over a hill. Life force, the ka. Order and truth, the goddess Maat. And powerful.

Put two or three of these in front of Ra and you have a throne name. “The form of Ra endures” is Menkheperre, the throne name of Thutmose III. “Ra is the lord of order” is Nebmaatre, the throne name of Amenhotep III. Translations differ from book to book, because these little sentences can often be read in more than one way. The signs don't change.

Around the cartouches you'll find the same few phrases again and again. Before the name: “the good god” and “lord of the Two Lands”. After it, a wish: “given life”, often followed by “forever”. You can already read almost every sign in them.

Two notes. The cone-shaped loaf is new: it reads dj and means “give”. And in “forever”, ḏt, the flat strip of land at the bottom is a determinative.

8Capstone: the king list at Abydos

Seventy-six kings on one wall, and you can read them.

Around 1290 BC, King Seti I built a temple at Abydos, the holy city of Osiris. In one of its corridors he had a list carved: the names of the kings of Egypt before him, starting with the first and ending with himself. There are 76 cartouches in two long rows. Seti stands in front of them with his young son, the future Ramesses II, who still wears a prince's sidelock.

The list isn't a history book. It is an offering list. The kings named on it were to receive a share of the temple's offerings for all time, and above every cartouche stand the same signs: , “for King…”. You can read that already. The ripple of water is n, “for”, from Class 2, and the rest is the word for king.

Almost every name on the wall is a throne name, so almost every cartouche begins with the sun. That makes the wall a fair test of everything in Module A: the alphabet, the big signs, the echoes, and gods first.

Read it as an Egyptologist would, and watch for what is missing as well as what is there. Part of a second list, from the temple of Ramesses II nearby, is in the British Museum (EA 117).

Tip: give each sign its own value, in the order the signs are written. The reading order comes afterwards.

Now walk along the wall on your own. Each cartouche below is copied from the list, and none is one you've read above. Type each name the way it is read, with Ra at the end. The last two are names that Seti chose not to carve.
