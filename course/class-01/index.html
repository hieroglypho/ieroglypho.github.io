1Warm-up: think in sounds

Before any hieroglyphs: one mental switch.

The most important idea in this class is one you already use. Pictures can stand for sounds instead of things. A picture of a bee followed by the number four doesn't mean "insects, quantity four". Said out loud, it's be-four: before. This is called the rebus principle. The Egyptians built a whole writing system on it about 5,200 years ago.

2A language, not a code

What exactly are we learning to read?

For centuries people treated hieroglyphs as a secret code of symbols carrying hidden wisdom. They aren't. Hieroglyphic is simply the way the ancient Egyptians wrote down their language, as ordinary as the alphabet you're reading now. That has a practical consequence: to read hieroglyphs you learn Egyptian, its words and its grammar, not a list of symbol meanings.

Egyptian belongs to the Afroasiatic family, distantly related to Arabic, Hebrew and Berber. It was written for over 4,000 years, from around 3200 BC, and it changed a great deal over that time. Scholars divide its history into stages:

Old Egyptianca. 2600–2000 BC
Pyramid Texts, tomb biographies
Middle Egyptian ★ca. 2000–1300 BC as a spoken language, written on monuments until AD 394
Late Egyptianca. 1300–700 BC
letters, stories, the Ramesside age
Demoticca. 650 BC – AD 450
contracts, literature
Copticfrom ca. AD 200
written in Greek letters; still used in church

This course teaches Middle Egyptian. It was the "classical" form of the language: the language of the great stories (Sinuhe, The Shipwrecked Sailor) and the form scribes kept using on temple walls, stelae and statues for 2,000 years after people stopped speaking it. It works like Latin did in medieval Europe. Learn Middle Egyptian and most of the hieroglyphs you see in a museum become readable.

The word hieroglyph is Greek: hieros "sacred" + glyphein "to carve". The Egyptians' own name for their script was mdw-nṯr, "the god's words". (Grammar note: a single sign is a hieroglyph, and the writing system is hieroglyphic.)

The one surprise to accept now: hieroglyphic writing records consonants only. Vowels went unwritten, as they often do in Arabic and Hebrew today. So we don't know exactly how most words sounded. Egyptologists write words in a standard set of letters called transliteration (in this course, James P. Allen's system: ꜣ j y ꜥ w b p f m n r h ḥ ḫ ẖ z s š q k g t ṯ d ḏ). You'll learn them in Classes 2 and 3; hear them now if you're curious.

3The three jobs of a sign

Every hieroglyph is a picture, but a picture can do three different jobs.

pr “house”
prj “go out”
  1. Ideogram (word-sign): it means what it shows. The house plan written on its own is the word pr, "house". The little stroke underneath is the scribe's hint: take this picture literally.
  2. Phonogram (sound-sign): it stands for sounds. The word for "house" had the consonants p + r. So the house sign can also spell those sounds in any word, such as prj "to go out", which has nothing to do with houses. This is the rebus trick from the warm-up. The mouth (r) after it confirms the r.
  3. Determinative: a silent tag for meaning. The walking legs at the end aren't pronounced. They tell you what kind of word this is: something about movement. Determinatives usually come at the end of a word. Since hieroglyphic has no spaces, they double as handy word-dividers.

Determinatives are why Egyptian spelling is easier to read than it first looks. The same consonants can spell different words, and the tag tells them apart:

jmn “hidden, to hide”
Jmn “Amun”, the Hidden One, with the seated-god tag
A working Middle Egyptian text uses a few hundred signs (the standard reference list, Gardiner's, has about 750). Some are mostly sound-signs, some mostly ideograms or determinatives, and a few, like the house, do all three jobs. You won't memorise them as one big list. You'll pick them up a few at a time, by how often they appear.

4Which way do I read?

Left, right, or down? Yes.

Hieroglyphs can run in rows or columns, and rows can go right-to-left or left-to-right. The Egyptians preferred right-to-left. They switched freely when it looked better, for example to make two inscriptions mirror each other on either side of a doorway, or so that a caption faces the person it labels.

You never have to guess, because there's one rule. Signs with a face (people, animals, birds) look toward the start of the line. Read into their faces. Columns are always read top to bottom; use the faces to tell which column comes first.

faces look left → start at the left →
← start at the right ← faces look right

Modern books (and the ΙΕΡΟΓΛΥΦΩ editor by default) usually print hieroglyphs left-to-right for convenience. On real monuments you'll often meet the other direction, so let's train your eye.

5Signs in groups

Why hieroglyphs look like tidy blocks, and how to unpack them.

Scribes didn't write signs in a single file like letters. They packed them into neat, roughly square groups, avoiding gaps. Signs come in three basic shapes:

ShapeExamplesUsually…
tall stand alone, filling the full height
flat stack on top of each other
small tuck into gaps, beside or under others

Reading rule: inside a group, read top before bottom, and front before back (from the side the signs face). So in ꜥnḫ "life" you read the tall sign first, then , then the sign below it.

There are also no spaces and no punctuation. Atfirstthatsoundshard, but you're reading this sentence fine, because you know the words. Your vocabulary and the determinatives will do the same job for Egyptian.

6Case file: the decipherment

Now redo the most famous code-breaking in history yourself, one clue at a time.

After the last hieroglyphic inscription was carved (at Philae, AD 394), knowledge of the script died within a generation or two. A 5th-century book called the Hieroglyphica, attributed to Horapollo, "explained" the signs as mystical symbols: a goose meant "son" because geese love their young. (The real reason: the bird's name sounded like the word for son, zꜣ. It's a rebus.) That symbolic approach led scholars astray for 1,400 years.

Then, in 1799, French soldiers found a broken slab at Rosetta (el-Rashid) in the Nile Delta. The Rosetta Stone carries a priestly decree from 196 BC honouring King Ptolemy V, written three times: in hieroglyphic, in Demotic (the everyday Egyptian script of the time) and in Greek, which scholars could read. It's now in the British Museum (EA 24).

Your clue: royal names in hieroglyphic are enclosed in an oval ring, the cartouche. The Greek text keeps mentioning Ptolemaios; the hieroglyphic text keeps repeating one cartouche. Assume they match.

What Champollion heard vs. what we know now

Champollion matched signs to Greek letters. Two centuries of research have refined those values. Because Egyptian writes consonants, several signs he took for vowels are really consonants (and that changes nothing about how you read the cartouches). These are the values you'll use from now on:

7Drill: Sign Sprint

Fourteen signs, sixty seconds. Recognition has to become automatic.

Everything you deciphered becomes your first working sign set. Answers use Allen's transliteration. A streak of 5 doubles your points, and wrong answers break it, so speed and accuracy count. Play it more than once: each round mixes the signs differently, and that mixing is what trains your eye.
