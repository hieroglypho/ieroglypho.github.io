2Four kinds of h

English has one h. Egyptian had four, and the script keeps them apart.

You know the first one already: the courtyard is a plain h. The other three were made further back in the mouth and throat. Transliteration tells them apart with a mark under the letter: a dot, a small arc, or a line.

courtyardhh as in house"h"
twisted wick of flaxḥa stronger, breathier h pushed from the throat, like the h in Arabic Aḥmad"h"
striped disc (perhaps a placenta or a sieve)ḫa rough "kh", like ch in Scottish loch"kh"
belly and udder of an animalẖa softer "kh", like ch in German ich"kh"

The table lists the four h's. The courtyard is a plain h, as in house. The twisted wick is h with a dot below: a stronger, breathier h from the throat. The striped disc is h with an arc below: a rough kh, as in Scottish loch. The belly and udder is h with a line below: a softer kh, as in German ich. In class, the first two are said "h" and the last two "kh".

Say the same, write them differently. In class you say ḫ and ẖ both as "kh". But they are different letters, and they make different words: ḫt means "things, property", while ẖt means "belly, body". Both are said "khet". The sign tells you which one is meant, so always transliterate what you see.

Typing them: on a plain keyboard, H gives ḥ, x gives ḫ and X gives ẖ. Lower-case h stays a plain h.

3Hisses, stops and clicks

Nine letters, four of which you already know.

The rest of the alphabet comes in small families of related sounds: three hissing sounds, three k-sounds, two t's and two d's. You met the folded cloth s, the basket k, the bread loaf t and the hand d in Class 1. Each one now gets its neighbours.

The table lists ten signs. The door bolt is z, said "s" in class. The folded cloth is s. The pool is sh. The sandy hill slope is q, a k made further back in the throat. The basket is k. The jar stand is a hard g. The bread loaf is t. The tethering rope is tj, like ch in church. The hand is d. The cobra is dj, like j in jam.

Two s's. In the oldest Egyptian the door bolt and the folded cloth were different sounds, probably "z" and "s". By the time of Middle Egyptian they had merged, and scribes often wrote either sign. Transliteration still keeps them apart, following the oldest spellings. That's why this course writes the word for "son", which you met in Class 1, as zꜣ: its earliest spellings use the bolt. You still say it "sa".

Sounds on the move. Over the centuries many ṯ's turned into plain t and many ḏ's into d, so you'll sometimes find one word spelled both ways. Transliterate the sign that's actually written.

Typing them: z, S for š, q, T for ṯ, D for ḏ. The rule is the same as for the h's: a capital letter adds the mark.

4The whole alphabet, in order

Twenty-five letters, the order every dictionary uses.

Here are all the one-consonant signs together. This week's new ones have a gold border.

In dictionary order: aleph, yod, y, ayin, w; b, p, f, m; n, r; the four h's; z, s, sh; q, k, g; t, tj, d, dj.

The order is a modern convention, not an ancient one, but Egyptologists' dictionaries, word lists and indexes follow it. It isn't random. It runs in families of sounds, which makes it easy to learn in chunks:

  • ꜣ j y ꜥ w: the weak consonants
  • b p f m: made with the lips
  • n r
  • h ḥ ḫ ẖ: the four h's
  • z s š: the hisses
  • q k g: the k-sounds
  • t ṯ d ḏ: the t's and d's

Sorting works as in English: compare the first letters; if they're the same, compare the second, and so on. So rn comes before rḫ, because n comes before ḫ.

5Phonetic complements

Why some sounds seem to be written twice.

Look again at two words from Class 1, now that you know the alphabet. ꜥnḫ "life" starts with the ankh, a sign that stands for all three consonants ꜥ, n and ḫ by itself. Yet under it come the water n and the striped disc ḫ. In jmn "hidden", the game board already says mn, and the water n follows it again.

These extra one-consonant signs are called phonetic complements. They repeat sounds that the bigger sign already contains, usually its last one or two, so the reader can be sure which sign it is and how to read it. You read them once, not twice: ꜥnḫ, never "ankh-n-kh".

nfr + f + r = nfr “good, beautiful”
ḥtp + t + p = ḥtp “offering; to be at peace”

A rule of thumb: when a one-consonant sign repeats a sound of the big sign just before it, it's a complement. Signs that stand for two or three consonants are next week's subject, so for now you don't need to know them. Just learn to spot the echo, because it often tells you what the big sign says.

6Reading by sound II

Real words, now with all 25 letters in play.

The method hasn't changed: follow the faces to find the start, give each sound-sign its letter, read top before bottom, and skip the determinative. What's new is that you now need the marks under the letters. A plain h where the word has ḫ is a spelling mistake, so look at each h-sign carefully.

Two determinatives turn up again and again in this set. The man with his hand to his mouth tags words about speaking, eating, thinking, and also keeping quiet. The walking legs tag words about movement.

And the other way round. Build each word from the palette, which now holds all the look-alikes side by side.

7Case file: pyramid builders and a boy king

Two kings of the pyramid age, their god, and a king who lived twelve centuries later.

Around 2550 BC, Egypt's kings built the largest stone monuments of the ancient world. The greatest of them, the Great Pyramid at Giza, belonged to a king whose name you can now read with the alphabet alone. His father before him had built two giant pyramids at Dahshur, a little further south.

About 1,200 years later, a young king was buried in a small tomb in the Valley of the Kings, far to the south at Thebes. When Howard Carter opened it in 1922, the king's name was written on almost everything inside.

New this week: two of these names use a sign for several consonants, together with its phonetic complements. The big sign is labelled for you; your job is the rest.

9Editor missions

Write your own name the way an Egyptian would hear it, then set the builders' names.

Open ΙΕΡΟΓΛΥΦΩ in a second window. In Class 1 you wrote your name "Ptolemaic style", with signs standing in for vowels, the way Greek names were spelled more than two thousand years after the pyramids. Now that you know the alphabet, you can do it the older way: consonants only.
