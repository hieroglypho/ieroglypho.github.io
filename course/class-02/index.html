1Warm-up: consonants are enough

First, pull last week back out of memory. Then a quick experiment.

Hieroglyphic writing leaves out vowels. That sounds like a serious handicap until you try it. Txt wtht vwls s stll qt rdbl, because consonants carry most of the information in a word. Your brain fills the gaps from what it already knows. Egyptian readers did exactly that.

2Transliteration

One fixed letter for every Egyptian consonant.

When Egyptologists write an Egyptian word in our alphabet, they don't guess at vowels. They copy the consonants, one letter per sound, using a fixed set of 25 letters. This is called transliteration. It's how dictionaries are ordered, how grammars quote words, and how you'll take notes. The word for "name", for example, is written with a mouth and a ripple of water, and transliterated rn.

Egyptian had more consonants than the Latin alphabet has letters: four kinds of h, two of s, two of t, two of d. So some letters carry dots, hooks or lines: ḥ ḫ ẖ š ṯ ḏ. Two sounds have their own symbols that look like half-rings: ꜣ (aleph) and ꜥ (ayin). This week you'll learn twelve of the 25. The others, with all the dotted letters, come in Class 3.

How to say it out loud

A word like rn has no vowels, so how do you pronounce it in class? Egyptologists use a simple convention. It isn't how the Egyptians spoke, but it lets people talk to each other:

  • Put a short e between consonants: rn is said "ren", nfr "good" is said "nefer".
  • Say ꜣ and ꜥ as a: ꜥnḫ "life" is "ankh".
  • Say j as i and w as u when they sit between consonants or at the end of a word.
The real sound was different. Coptic, the last stage of Egyptian, still had the word for "good": noufe. So the ancient word had vowels and a shape that "nefer" doesn't capture. Treat the classroom pronunciation as a handle, not a reconstruction.

Typing it. You don't need special keys. In this course (and in the ΙΕΡΟΓΛΥΦΩ editor and dictionary) you can type plain-keyboard codes: A for ꜣ, i for j, a for ꜥ. Other letters are typed as themselves. Note that many books and the Hieroglyphica dictionary write the reed leaf as i; this course follows Allen and writes j. Same sign, two conventions.

3Five weak consonants

The signs beginners mistake for vowels.

The Egyptian alphabet, in dictionary order, starts with five sounds that were all "weak": soft consonants that tend to fade, merge with vowels or drop out of a word over time. Because of that, Champollion and the scholars after him took several of them for vowels, and you still see those old values in popular books. In real Egyptian they are consonants, and you transliterate them every time.

The table shows the five signs. The vulture is aleph, said "a" in class; its ancient sound is debated. The flowering reed is yod, said "i". Two reeds, or two short strokes at the end of a word, are y. The forearm is ayin, a deep throat sound, said "a". The quail chick, or its shorthand, a coil of rope, is w, said "w" or "u".

Tell the birds apart. Egyptian has many bird signs, and three of the most common are sound-signs you'll meet in nearly every text: the vulture (ꜣ), with its hooked beak and long back; the quail chick (w), small and round with no tail to speak of; and the owl (m), coming next, which turns its face toward you.

One reed or two? A single reed leaf is j. Two reed leaves side by side are not "j j" but one sound, y. You'll see this at the end of a famous royal name in the case file.

4Seven strong consonants

These sound just like their English letters.

The next seven signs, in dictionary order, are easy to hear: b p f m n r h. You already know three of them from Ptolemy and Cleopatra. Learn each one with its picture, because the picture is your memory hook.

SignPictureLetterMemory hook
lower leg and footbb for boot

The table gives each sign a memory hook. The foot is b, for boot. The stool is p, the first sign of Ptolemy. The horned viper is f, for fangs; after a word it also means "his". The owl is m, the bird that looks at me; on its own, m means "in" or "with". The ripple of water is n, for Nile; on its own it means "to" or "for", and the red crown can write it too. The mouth is r; on its own, r means "to" or "toward". The plan of a courtyard is a plain h, as in house.

Notice that the owl, the water and the mouth also work as tiny words on their own. They are some of the most frequent signs in any text, so learning them pays off at once.

5Reading by sound

Put the signs together and words come out.

Many Egyptian words are spelled entirely with one-consonant signs, often followed by a silent determinative. Reading them takes four steps:

  1. Find the start: follow the faces, as in Class 1.
  2. Read each sound-sign in order, top before bottom inside a group, and write down its letter.
  3. Two reed leaves together give one y.
  4. Skip the determinative and any strokes at the end. They aren't pronounced, but they tell you what kind of word you have.
p + t + the sky sign = pt “sky”
r + m + w + a fish + plural strokes = rmw “fish”

Say them the classroom way: "pet", "remu". The determinatives make these words easy to spot in a long line of text: a sky stretched over the top, a fish swimming at the end.

Now go the other way. Writing a word from its sounds is harder than reading it, and that's why it works: producing the answer fixes the signs in memory much better than recognising them.

6Case file: a family at Saqqara

Three royal names, spelled only with the signs you know.

Saqqara is the great cemetery of Memphis, Egypt's old capital, just south of modern Cairo. Around 2300 BC, a king called Teti founded a new ruling family there, the Sixth Dynasty, and built his pyramid at the site. His queen, Iput, was buried in a small pyramid next to his. Their son became King Pepi I.

In 1880, workers entered Pepi's pyramid and found its inner rooms covered with columns of hieroglyphs: the Pyramid Texts, spells to help the king in the afterlife and among the oldest religious writings anywhere. Teti's burial chamber held the same kind of inscriptions. Your job is to read the family's names in them.

Why they're easy: these names are spelled entirely with one-consonant signs. Old Kingdom names were often short, and scribes wrote them sound by sound.
